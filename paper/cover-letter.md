# Cover letter (Manuscript Central)

**To:** Editor-in-Chief, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction

**Manuscript:** Spoken Compound Notation: Using Sanskrit Samāsa to Represent Mathematical Operators in Sound

## Why TOCHI

The manuscript is an HCI design paper about notation as an interface object, not a computational-linguistics paper about Sanskrit and not a programming-languages paper about APL. It argues that Iverson’s operators and Pāṇinian compound formation are the same kind of generative device, then builds a spoken operator language and a hold-to-talk keyboard on that mapping. The archival question is whether sonic notation can inherit the economy and suggestivity of a written operator system instead of flattening operators into English paraphrases. That question belongs next to TOCHI’s work on speech interaction, ability-based design, and notations as cognitive artifacts, and next to CHI/UIST/ASSETS work on programming by voice and mathematical accessibility.

## Scope

The paper contributes (1) a design theory mapping Iverson (1980) onto Satuluri and Kulkarni’s ICON 2013 compound generator, (2) a stem-and-join language for 119 array-language glyphs, (3) a working speaker-dependent keyboard, and (4) a three-study evaluation protocol. It does **not** report a completed user study. Section 10 is written so the studies can be run without redesigning the contribution. A single-speaker observation from the running prototype (a 0.007 cosine collision between *yugma* and *adhosīmā*) is reported as a design implication, not as an experiment.
